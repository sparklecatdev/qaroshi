# Qaroshi cultural conventions and literary register

Qaroshi’s native script and obligatory written resonances connect the literal content of a message to the social circumstances of saying it. This chapter is an **editorial cultural reference**: it does not assert independently documented ancient history, regional dialects, or a natural speaker population.

## 1. Roost and chosen family

`naro` names a home or nesting place, while `runa` identifies chosen family. The distinction is practical: a shared building need not be chosen family, and chosen family need not share the same roost. Care is expressed through acts as readily as words. `më mi ti-m nashi.` expresses deep love or care; the register is affectionate but does not automatically establish romance or sexuality.

## 2. Flight as social space

The wings and flight lexicon offers a natural source for figurative meaning: keeping something beneath a wing can image quiet protection, and addressing the sky can image a public declaration. These are metaphors **under review**, not asserted traditional proverbs. A good dictionary must distinguish literal flight, technical flight terminology, and contextual speech images.

## 3. Stars, memory, and promises

The vocabulary `sëra` (star), `moru` (memory), and `namu` (promise) supports poems and solemn declarations. `qor mi ti-m daru-yu.` communicates an emphatic commitment, but a solemn register alone is not a legal contract. Writers should specify who promises what, to whom, and under what conditions.

## 4. Written emotional transparency

The sixteen mandatory written resonance markers are grammatical particles, not typographic emoji. Every independent written sentence gets exactly one at the start. Warm Hum (`më`), Bright Trill (`zha`), Deep Resonance (`qor`), Still Voice (`sen`) and other registers mark the intended emotional frame, not empirical truth. A story with a changed resonance must begin a new sentence or quotation.

## 5. Autonomy and consent

Ability `-ki`, permission `-ho` and obligation `-du` are distinct. Warm affection cannot override someone’s refusal or transform a request into permission. Written communication should be explicit when boundaries, safety and relationships are involved. Honorific and romantic interpretations should not be inferred from grammatical gender because Qaroshi has none.

## 6. Stories, myths and chronologies

Fiction, educational scenarios and community documentation belong in separate provenance categories. A fictional roost festival can be part of a story without being misrepresented as a historical event. Contributors proposing a dated language history should supply sources rather than retroactively create a chronology.

## 7. Literary editing protocol

For each new story include: source-language Qaroshi text; English translation; IPA; author/editing provenance; a description of metaphorical usages; a record of disputed grammatical readings; and review decisions. The current controlled corpus is suitable for exercises and reference, but it is not a body of speaker-observed natural literature.
