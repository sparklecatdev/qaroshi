# Qaroshi Living Speech: A Reference to Informality, Intimacy and Taboo

The ordinary dictionary gives headwords. This guide explains **when to use them**, how the same concept can be expressed differently in medical and informal settings, and how to avoid mistaking a written resonance marker for an expletive. The material includes explicit adult anatomical language, strong profanity, insults and discussions of consent. Those words are searchable just like any other vocabulary.

## 1. Speech styles versus resonance

Qaroshi distinguishes **resonance** (the compulsory first word in every complete sentence) from **register** (whether the vocabulary is neutral, intimate, clinical, coarse, colloquial, or technical). A sentence can be emotionally neutral and contain explicit clinical terminology. Equally, a warm-hum sentence can include a joking swear. Neither resonance nor informality is interchangeable with consent, seriousness or social respect.

| Written marker | Appropriate tone | A key distinction |
|---|---|---|
| `sen` | neutral, descriptive | Suitable for factual anatomy and medical discussion |
| `më` | warm, affectionate | Does not itself imply romantic or sexual consent |
| `zha` | playful, excited | Can accompany teasing; not automatically an invitation |
| `rak` | angry, defiant | Does not automatically mean the speaker swore |
| `qor` | solemn, emphatic | Often useful for firm boundaries and serious warnings |
| `hë` | sincerely inquisitive | A question still normally ends in `he` |
| `lën` | bashful, embarrassed | Can soften a sensitive subject without changing its meaning |

**Example:** `rak rakhë!` communicates an angry expletive. The *first* word is resonance; the *second* carries the profanity. `sen rakhë!` retains the expletive but gives it a comparatively matter-of-fact delivery. Always use one opening resonance, including in text messages.

## 2. Formality, offensiveness and context

- **Neutral**: everyday nouns, actions, questions and ordinary descriptions. No implied vulgarity.
- **Clinical**: anatomical terms, sexual health, hygiene, reproduction and treatment. Directness is not automatically vulgarity.
- **Adult/intimate**: romantic and sexual meanings that may be appropriate between consenting adults or in a relevant educational discussion. Use precise, non-assumptive wording.
- **Slang**: informal expressions influenced by group, place and generation. Literal translations are often misleading.
- **Vulgar**: strong swearing, coarse anatomical slang, insults and profanity. These may be offensive even among friends.
- **Technical**: research, legal, computing and other professional terms. Precision matters more than rhythm.

A speaker can use vulgar words *about a situation* without insulting anyone, or direct an insulting term at a person. Dictionary entries marked vulgar are not advice to address people that way. For protected identities, the lexicon describes respectful identity terms and discrimination; it does not require a repertoire of discriminatory slurs.

## 3. Anatomy versus colloquial terms

Body terminology is usually described in the clinical register. Colloquial anatomical expressions can be playful, crude, affectionate, or offensive depending on the relationship and setting. The existence of a slang word does not make it a suitable substitute in health or consent contexts. At clinics, reporting symptoms or asking about contraception, prefer precise terms.

The dictionary distinguishes genital anatomy from sexual activity, attraction from identity, pregnancy from fertility, and romantic intimacy from sexual intimacy. There are also distinct terms for contraception, STI tests, protective barriers, sexual autonomy and privacy.

## 4. Consent and boundaries are grammatical statements

The `nu` negator immediately precedes the verb, while `ya` closes a request or prohibition. The particle `he` closes a question. These constructions can express refusal and boundaries without profane language.

A refusal is a refusal regardless of the resonance used. The Warm Hum register is not a substitute for permission, nor does a relationship imply perpetual consent. In sensitive contexts, clear communication matters more than figurative or euphemistic phrasing.

**Patterns:**

- `qor ti nu VERB ya.` → “Do not VERB.”
- `hë ti NOUN-m hari he?` → “Do you have NOUN?”
- `dal mi VERB-yu.` → “I will VERB.”
- `sen mi NOUN-m hari.` → “I have NOUN.”

`VERB` and `NOUN` in the patterns above are grammatical placeholders, not Qaroshi words.

## 5. Swearing as pragmatics

Strong curses serve different functions: physical frustration, surprise, emphasis, joking solidarity, anger, and dismissal. A single expletive can mean very different things depending on tone and context. The dictionary treats many common exclamations as lexicalized interjections so their interpretation is stored rather than mechanically assembled.

Use the resonance marker to express the intended emotional delivery. A cheerful `zha` exclamation and an angry `rak` exclamation do not carry the same social force. Some insults have a target; others describe an event or object. In professional situations, substitute a neutral word for annoyance or disbelief rather than assuming vulgar wording is welcome.

## 6. Sexual vocabulary: relevant but precise

For adult contexts, the lexicon covers sexual anatomy, sexual orientation, romantic orientation, libido, arousal, masturbation, partnered sexual activity, contraceptives, prevention, boundaries, erotic expression, and the distinction between explicit media and intimate communication. Clinical terms are appropriate in health contexts; vulgar slang has different social force and should not be used as an accidental substitute.

Terms for coercion, harassment and assault are included for accurate reporting and discussion of boundaries. They do **not** function as synonyms for consensual intimacy. A word describing sexual identity does not tell you whether a person is sexually active, interested in a specific person, or comfortable being addressed in a particular way.

## 7. Slang and internet language

A word for a meme, a quoted sarcastic phrase, or a “ratio” is not necessarily meaningful outside the setting where it originated. Informal social-media terms are described by their intended *sense*, not by the English platform or orthographic form. Technical and slang uses may coexist in the dictionary when they have different meanings.

Older idioms should be interpreted by context, not assembled by translating their individual English component words. For example, a slang exclamation equivalent to “No way!” is a single lexical unit, while a literal denial normally uses `nu` or another grammatical construction.

## 10. Translation checklist

1. Identify literal meaning before choosing how strong or explicit the word should be.
2. Choose the correct *speech register*: clinical, neutral, affectionate, colloquial, vulgar or technical.
3. Place exactly one written resonance marker at the beginning of the complete sentence.
4. Apply the canonical case markers and verb endings; do not imitate English word order.
5. Check any question for the final `he`, and requests or prohibitions for `ya`.
6. Distinguish quoted obscenities from obscenities addressed to another person.
7. Avoid interpreting words for sexuality, gender or relationship structure as a claim about a specific individual.
8. Check the dictionary headword page for pronunciation, gloss, notes and native script.

## 11. Continued reference work

This volume covers vocabulary and usage conventions; it is not a source of sociolinguistic survey frequencies, dialect distributions, or historical attestation. Disputed context and local nuance can be annotated in dictionary notes without changing the original root form. Cross-language equivalence of insults is approximate because cultural offensiveness cannot be inferred mechanically from lexical definitions alone.
