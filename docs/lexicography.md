# Qaroshi Lexicography and Morphological Productivity

## Three tiers of lexical documentation

**Established** forms are preserved from Standard Reference 1.0 with no spelling, word-class, or English-gloss mutation. **Standardized** grammatical words are explicitly interpreted by the present grammar, such as `wënu` (contrary-to-fact if) and `qinë` (counterfactual result). **Candidate** lexical headwords are fixed, dictionary-indexed sense proposals, not automatically equivalent to spontaneous, community-attested usage. A candidate's English gloss describes the intended lexical sense, not necessarily every valid English translation of the word.

All 6,819 entries are unique in Romanized spelling. There are 853 established entries, 24 new grammatical function entries, and 5,942 new candidate lexical entries. The latter include 234 transparent compounds and 5,708 atomic headwords. The English index contains multiple aliases for established semicolon-separated definitions, making its entry count larger than the number of unique Qaroshi forms.

## Polysemy and sense boundaries

A word can have more than one meaning, but unrelated senses must not silently collapse together. For instance `charge (electric)`, `charge money`, and `charge (accusation)` differ in their syntactic behavior and domain; they should be given separate entries or explicitly documented as multiple senses only if evidence justifies the same form. Abstract nominal concepts such as `model (mathematical)`, `model (engineering)` and `model (AI)` may share a metaphorical origin but are not automatically identical.

Dictionary searches return **glosses**, not complete contextual translation. The Romanized form is the lemma. The original 26 glyphs encode its pronunciation, but do not themselves indicate its English meaning.

## Phonotactic coherence

The source orthography has six vowels, `a,e,i,o,u,ë`, and twenty consonantal glyphs, including the letters Romanized as `sh,zh,kh,ng`. Candidate forms use syllabic patterns compatible with `(C)(r/l/w/y)V(C)` and generally favor open three-syllable words. Their syllable-family initial is a vocabulary-organizing convention, not a grammatical affix, and must not be translated independently. Two forms beginning with the same syllable are **not** automatically synonyms.

The 2.0 lexical-term list was curated by semantic field. Candidate Romanized headwords are reproducibly assigned and locked; this procedural origin is a practical publishing safeguard, **not proof of natural historical word usage**. Future community usage may warrant better forms, but documented 1.0 vocabulary remains stable.

## Composition versus inflection

Nouns follow `ROOT + derivation + endearment + plural + case + topic`. Verbs follow `ROOT + voice + tense + aspect + modality + tender manner`. These inflected words are normally **not separate dictionary headwords**, avoiding misleading vocabulary-count inflation. A learner can form `mara-ri` (“friends”) and `goro-te` (“flew”) without adding two unrelated lexical entries.

A compound follows **modifier + head**. For example, `qaroshi` = `qaro` (Nardoragon) + `shi` (language), while a technical compound may concatenate two documented 2.0 roots. Compounds include a `components` field, so a lexicographer can review their transparency. A lexicalized compound can diverge in meaning from its parts, in which case that conventional usage requires an explicit entry note.

## Meaning, argument structure, and valency

Transitive verbs take object `-m`; an additional recipient normally takes `-tu`, and an instrument `-shi`. The English verb gloss sometimes names a typical object (“reset a password”), but the native verb still receives an explicit object when grammatically transitive. Real usage examples should annotate each predicate's expected participants. The current candidate inventory does not yet contain a complete valency dictionary; this is a priority for future editions.

## Borrowings and proper names

A proper name, URL, file path or code token can be left as quoted foreign content. Do not infer a Qaroshi phonetic adaptation from English spelling alone. For a truly lexicalized borrowing, supply a pronunciation, segmented 26-glyph mapping, and examples showing its grammatical behavior. Identity names are not automatically translated.

## Mismatches to resolve

1. High-frequency everyday words need corpus frequency and short-form preference work.
2. Some newly assigned roots have similar sound patterns; lexical confusion testing with users is incomplete.
3. Some machine-generated English reverse-index matches reflect a broad gloss and need sense-specific refinement.
4. Controlled example patterns are grammatical demonstrations, not recordings of ordinary spontaneous dialogue.
5. Regional and conversational colloquial variants remain largely undocumented.
6. Morphological productivity should be tested on unfamiliar roots, not only common examples.

A dictionary suitable for modern academic and everyday publishing needs attestation, editorial examples, revision history and a semantic network, not merely an expanded number of lemmas.
