# Qaroshi Translation Guide

## From English to Qaroshi

1. Identify each independent sentence and its intended social/emotional **resonance**. Neutral facts use `sen`, sincere questions often `hë`, tender speech `më`, serious promises `qor`; the set has sixteen options.
2. Identify subjects, direct objects, recipients, instruments, locations, time phrases, and subordinate clauses. Mark direct objects `-m`, recipients `-tu`, places `-lo` or other relevant case.
3. Put the finite predicate after its normal arguments. Attach at most one tense, aspect, voice, and modality suffix in the documented order.
4. Put `nu` immediately before the whole predicate if it is being negated.
5. Use appropriate connectors (`ro`, `ko`, `wë`, `wënu`, `dasha`, `shoru`, `qona`) without accidentally starting a second register in a dependent clause.
6. Finish with a question or request marker if the speech act requires one: `he` for ordinary direct questions, `hëmi` for confirmation questions, or `ya` for commands/requests.
7. Read the result back literally, verifying scope and argument roles, then edit for natural style. The dictionary is an aid to composition, not an automatic translation guarantee.

## From Qaroshi to English

First identify resonance, then segment the clause. Case suffixes determine which participant is doing or receiving an action. Tense, aspect, modality and negation specify the predicate's temporal/semantic interpretation. Evidentials (`si`, `hu`, `ye`, `lëshu`) should be translated when relevant but do not necessarily require awkward literal wording. Next, reorder the English translation into ordinary English clause order without erasing distinctions such as inclusive/exclusive `we` or direct-vs-counterfactual conditionals.

## Worked examples

**Affection:** `më ti lësha.` = “You are cute.” `më` marks warmth; no overt present-tense copula is needed.

**Urgent reassurance:** `khar ti haro he?` = “Are you okay?!” `khar` conveys alarm while terminal `he` marks the question.

**Past progressive:** `sen sa goro-te-se.` = “They were flying.” Root `goro` + past `-te` + ongoing aspect `-se`.

**Recipient:** `sen mi mara-tu kima-m pëli.` = “I give water to a friend.” `-tu` marks the recipient, and `-m` the transferred thing.

**Counterfactual:** `sen wënu mi goro-ki, mi wena-gu gura-yu qinë.` = “If I could fly, I would travel into the sky.” `wënu` identifies an unreal premise; `qinë` closes the hypothetical result.

**Wish:** `më yëmë ti haro.` = “May you be safe.” A wish is not the same as an order. `ya` is not present.

**Embedded question:** `sen sa haro qona mi sëmi.` = “I know whether they are well.” `qona` embeds an informational alternative; no embedded `he` is required.

**Longing:** `wen mi ti-m nashi.` = “I cherish you deeply.” The register `wen` frames the feeling as longing; `nashi` itself denotes deep care, not necessarily romance.

**Canonical plea:** `më pëla, ti sëna nu zëru ya, ko ti mi-wa sëna nëra-ki.` = “Please never die, so you can stay with me forever.” The opening `më` covers both clauses; `sëna nu zëru` means “always not die”; `ko` introduces the purpose; `mi-wa` means “with me”; `nëra-ki` is “can stay.”

## Ambiguity caution

An exact English sentence does not always have one universal equivalent. The user may intend a hypothetical possibility, a real future plan, a command, a solemn wish, an affectionate compliment, or irony. The written resonance register makes intended stance explicit, but lexical meanings and context still matter. Avoid forcing a novel technical meaning onto an existing familiar Qaroshi root just because its English gloss seems similar.
