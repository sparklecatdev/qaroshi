# Qaroshi translation protocol: from English concepts to accurate sentences

Translation is a sequence of semantic decisions, not word-by-word substitution. The procedure below is designed for the unified standard, including the 30,000-entry dictionary, the 16 resonance markers and the advanced syntax additions.

## 1. Determine the speaker's act

Is this an assertion, question, command, request, quotation, hypothesis, promise, wish or fragment? A direct question will ordinarily end with `he`, a command/request with `ya`. A quotation may contain an embedded independently marked sentence. The English original does not always expose these distinctions, so do not invent an imperative when the source is only a statement.

## 2. Choose the written resonance

Use `sen` for neutral factual prose when no specific stance is requested. `më` for warm, `hë` for curiosity, `qor` for solemnity, etc., only if that nuance belongs to the source context. The typed marker is not a translation of “I feel,” and a serious warning is not always anger. Every independent Qaroshi sentence must have exactly one such opening marker.

## 3. Establish the semantic predicate and argument slots

Identify who acts, what is acted on, whether there is a recipient, location, source, destination, instrument or companion. Select the lexical verb sense from the structured semantic frame table when available. Some English phrasal verbs have no one-word equivalent; use a descriptive clause instead of a guessed compound.

Example: “I give water to a friend” becomes `sen mi mara-tu kima-m pëli.` The recipient has `-tu`, the thing given `-m`, and the predicate closes the clause.

## 4. Build the noun phrases

Translate possession using `-na` on the possessor (`mi-na naro`), mark a definite direct object with `-m` on its head, attach adjective after noun, and keep numerals after nouns. Don't assume an English “a/the” distinction must be explicitly encoded. Include plural `-ri` when plurality matters and is not already clear from counting or context.

## 5. Order the verbal endings

`ROOT + voice + tense + aspect + modality + tender manner`. At most one voice, one tense, one aspect and one modality in an ordinary finite predicate. Example: `sen sa goro-te-se.` = They were flying. Do not generate `goro-se-te` or `goro-te-yu`.

## 6. Attach negation and focus

`nu` goes directly before its predicate. A focus-sensitive word such as `ngëshi` (only) has its own constituent scope; don't mistake “I only saw the friend” for “I saw only the friend.” If the scope is unclear, rewrite the sentence as two simpler statements.

## 7. Build the clause hierarchy

Use `wë` for an if-clause, `ro` for a reason, `ko` for a purpose, `do` for a proposition and a clause ending in `sha` before the noun for a relative. Keep a single resonance for the encompassing sentence, not for each dependent clause. If multiple embeddings force an ambiguous parse, divide the thought into independent sentences, each with its own register.

## 8. Decide how evidence is represented

Does the speaker personally know, remember, infer, measure or quote the claim? Use a documented evidential only when warranted. `hu` (reported) is not interchangeable with `si` (witnessed); `ye` (inferred) is not a magic “counterfactual” marker. Avoid adding a certainty particle to satisfy an English rhetorical flourish unless the source actually conveys that confidence.

## 9. Preserve taboo level and social boundaries

A request for intimacy is not the same as consent to an intimate act; direct refusal must remain unambiguous. Clinical language is not necessarily vulgar, and profanity can be a general exclamation or an interpersonal insult. Translate the **intended register and target**, not merely the nearest obscene English gloss.

## 10. Produce phonetics in two layers

For each finished utterance, compute broad IPA using the established 26-glyph inventory, and add a beginner-friendly stress-marked reading. Do not claim the English-style cue is a native pronunciation recording. The full text must also render to native glyphs with longest-match handling for `sh`, `zh`, `kh`, `ng`.

## 11. Back-translate

Before publishing, translate your Qaroshi result back into English **without consulting the original sentence**. Ask which entailments were lost or added. For example `sen sa goro-ho.` back-translates as “they are permitted to fly,” not “they are able to fly.” `më mi ti-m nashi.` is “I cherish you,” not necessarily “I desire you sexually.”

## 12. Formal verification checklist

Verify: `[ ]` resonance count, `[ ]` subject reference, `[ ]` selected verb frame, `[ ]` case roles, `[ ]` suffix order, `[ ]` negation adjacency, `[ ]` question/imperative final, `[ ]` evidence source, `[ ]` quantifier scope, `[ ]` stance match, `[ ]` native glyph segmentability, `[ ]` IPA and sound-out, `[ ]` consistent terminology across paragraphs. A high-quality translation can prioritize clarity over superficial concision.

## 13. Worked examples

**English:** My throat is dry.

**Qaroshi:** `sen mi-na gëro dërawa.`

**IPA:** /sen ˈmi.na ˈɡə.ɾo də.ˈɾa.wa/

**Reading:** sen MI-na GË-ro dë-RA-wa.

**Parse:** `sen` neutral resonance; `mi-na` my; `gëro` throat; `dërawa` predicate dry. Present property predicates need no English copula.

**English:** Please never die so you can stay with me forever.

**Qaroshi:** `më pëla, ti sëna nu zëru ya, ko ti mi-wa sëna nëra-ki.`

**Parse:** `më` warm sentence resonance; `pëla` politeness; `sëna nu zëru` always not die; `ya` request act; `ko` purpose; `mi-wa` with me; `nëra-ki` can remain. The main sentence's register extends through the purpose clause.

**English:** I will protect you.

**Qaroshi:** `qor mi ti-m daru-yu.`

**Parse:** solemn resonance; first-person subject; second-person object; future protection. It is a promise, not proof it will succeed.
