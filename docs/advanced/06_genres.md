# Genre conventions: dialogue, narrative, debate and technical exposition

Different kinds of texts pressure a grammar in different ways. Everyday exchanges depend on pronoun resolution and repair. Stories need tense perspective and long-distance reference. Scientific reports distinguish inference from measurement. Legal and consent statements must be explicit about who is authorized or required to act. This chapter provides a genre-sensitive checklist using the same Qaroshi grammar throughout.

## A. Everyday conversation

Use a new resonance at every independent sentence boundary, even across short turns. In casual dialogue an omitted first-person pronoun is possible after a direct question, but never omit it when the addressee or referent changes. Greetings can be fragments in English, yet Qaroshi examples intended as complete utterances remain resonance-marked: `zha ha, mara!`. A question and reply are two sentences with potentially different stances: `hë ti haro he?` / `më mi haro.`.

**Realism test:** A six-turn exchange should contain at least one non-answer response, clarification, topic shift, refusal, or correction. A conversation where every question receives an overly perfect dictionary definition is grammatical but not realistic.

## B. Narrative

Establish the narrator's reference time. Past `-te` can anchor events before the current narrative time, while aspect distinguishes ongoing from completed states. The prose must distinguish background conditions from events advancing the plot: `sen sa naro-lo nëra-te-se.` (They were staying at home) vs `sen sa naro-gu gura-te.` (They went toward home). In a long narrative, repeat noun phrases often enough to prevent multiple singular `sa` antecedents from becoming confused.

A narrator can mark evidence (`si`, `hu`, `ye`) when it matters, but there is no requirement to add an evidential to every fictional sentence. The narrative's `sen` register does not preclude emotion inside reported dialogue; each quoted independent utterance can retain its own marker.

## C. Debate and argumentation

Separate **claim**, **reason**, **evidence**, **qualification**, **objection**, and **rebuttal**. Use `ro` for a reason and `nao` for contrast, not as interchangeable emphatic particles. When the speaker is uncertain, use `sëro`, appropriate evidential markers and/or `sëki`; do not fake certainty through a serious resonance. A fair rebuttal should paraphrase the opposing claim accurately before challenging it.

Avoid rhetorical ambiguity around scope: “not everyone” is not “nobody,” and permission is not ability. When an argument relies on either distinction, spell it out using explicit clauses. A serious stance `qor` should signal commitment to the reasoning, not an automatic proof.

## D. Technical instructions

A procedure needs an explicit actor or addressee and a recoverable order of operations. `ya` closes imperatives, while `-du` signals requirements in declaratives; they are not interchangeable. Every numbered step should be a full sentence with a resonance if written as a complete instruction. Identify instruments (`-shi`), locations (`-lo`), sources (`-de`), and goals (`-gu`) rather than using a generic “with” relation.

When there is risk, prioritize **unambiguous sequencing** over idiomatic terseness. Terms borrowed from another language should either be mapped to established Qaroshi lexical entries or left as labeled external quotations. The technical dictionary's large productive compound inventory is helpful, but a descriptive phrase may be clearer than a rare compound.

## E. Science and observational reporting

Distinguish direct observation `si`, measured/calculated outcome `wërusha`, report `hu`, written archive `rëhu`, and inference `ye`. “A sensor measured X” is not identical to “I saw X.” State measurement units and uncertainty explicitly. `sëki` is low confidence; `qëru` is high confidence, but neither substitutes for a confidence interval or experimental protocol. A report that claims “evidence proves” should name the evidence and method, not rely solely on the particle.

Every claim should be paired with its source type when the distinction matters to the conclusion. Some sentences can remain unmarked for evidence while still having the obligatory resonance. In translations of mathematical statements, maintain the exact quantifier and base of a numeral; Qaroshi's traditional base-six system must not silently change a decimal value.

## F. Law, consent and safety

Write explicit obligations and permissions: `-du` must/required, `-ho` permitted, `mahori` voluntary consent, `noharo` refusal or boundary vocabulary. A request phrased warmly remains a request. A negative answer remains negative regardless of the resonance particle. If a consequence depends on a condition, use a clearly bounded `wë` or `bëna` clause and identify which obligation depends on it.

Avoid ambiguity in number, person, object, scope, authority and deadline. A translator should not upgrade a **possibility** to a **commitment** or a **social preference** to a **legal obligation**. When the existing grammar leaves two parses possible, write two short sentences.

## G. Literature, poetry and humor

Poetry can omit subjects for rhythm, create transparent new compounds, exploit repeated register contrasts, or use metaphorical frames. Such creative license does not change the canonical semantics of a lexical form in the reference dictionary. An idiom used in literal form may become a pun; explain both readings when teaching the line. The native script's drawn strokes lend themselves to visual composition, but the original glyph distinctions still need to be recognizable.

**Editorial policy:** mark deliberate nonstandard usage in literary annotations instead of teaching it as a generally required rule.

## H. Dialogue annotation

For a connected dialogue, annotate each line with speaker, addressee, intent, stance, referents, whether the information is new or given, and whether the line is an answer or repair. Then test the whole interaction: what does each participant know after their turn? Who has consented? What promise, if any, is still outstanding? Can pronouns still be resolved two turns later?

## I. Translation practice

A robust translation exercise should include a source text, a context note, a natural Qaroshi rendering, a back-translation, and a brief statement of what meaning is intentionally **not** implied. For example `qor mi ti-m daru-yu.` promises protection but does not report that the protection succeeded. `sen sa goro-ki.` indicates flight capability but not permission. `më pëla mi-wa nëra ya.` requests companionship without establishing consent to anything else.

## J. Coverage boundaries

These genre guidelines are an editorial account of *standard expressive resources*, not a claim that every naturalistic text type has already been independently observed. Connected texts in the corpus are controlled compositions used to uncover grammatical gaps. The test suite can validate structure and data integrity; a future human editorial process is still needed to assess tone, naturalness and ambiguity.
