# Scope, logic, modality and counterfactual meaning

Natural language relies on both **truth conditions** (what would make a proposition true) and **discourse conditions** (what it assumes, contrasts or implies). Qaroshi's resonance marker adds a second expressive layer but does **not** by itself change the truth conditions of the predicate. This guide makes the difference testable.

## 1. Negation as an operator

`nu` immediately before a predicate negates that predicate. `sen mi nu goro.` = I do not fly; `sen mi kima-m nu këmë.` = I do not drink water. The position is not equivalent to a scope-neutral English “not” floating anywhere in a sentence. `zëna` = never, literally no time. `sëna nu V` = always not V; both can express never, but the second emphasizes sustained absence in emotionally insistent statements.

`sen mi kima-m nu këmë.` does **not** establish that I drink something else. It says only that I do not drink water. `sen mi kima-m këmë.` does **not** establish that I drink only water. The stronger reading requires an explicit exclusivity marker and a clear domain.

## 2. Only, also, even

`ngëshi` follows a focused constituent and means “only”; `bëso` adds that constituent to known alternatives; `tëmiri` singles out an unexpected member. In focus-sensitive contexts, the set of plausible alternatives must be supplied by discourse. `sen dëna qaro ngëshi haro.` = Only this Nardoragon is well. It says other relevant candidates are not well; it is not a universal statement about every Nardoragon anywhere. `sen dëna qaro bëso haro.` = This Nardoragon is also well. `sen dëna qaro tëmiri haro.` = Even this Nardoragon is well.

A negative focus sentence can have two interpretations: “only X is not P” vs “it is not the case that only X is P.” Prefer two explicit sentences with the alternatives named, rather than relying on an untested linear scope hierarchy. The reference's practical principle is **make scope visible**.

## 3. Quantifiers, collectivity, distribution

Numerals follow nouns: `qaro po` = two Nardoragons. Quantifier bounds `qësho` (at least), `rëshu` (at most), `nëmi` (exactly) stand before the numeral: `qaro-ri qësho po` = at least two Nardoragons. This does not alone settle whether a group acts jointly or each member acts independently. To force the contrast, use documented collective/distributive particles (`gawo` / `nashu`) with an explanatory context and avoid interpreting an unspecified plural as inherently collective.

A literal English “everyone didn't...” is dangerously ambiguous; translate the intended reading: *none acted* or *not all acted*. Do not depend on word order alone to decide logical quantifier scope in a consequential statement.

## 4. Epistemic vs deontic vs dynamic modality

Qaroshi distinguishes `-ki` ability (“can”), `-ho` permission (“may”), `-du` necessity (“must”). These are not interchangeable:

- `sen sa goro-ki.` = They can fly (ability or possibility based on capacity).
- `sen sa goro-ho.` = They are permitted to fly.
- `qor sa goro-du.` = They must fly (obligation/necessity).

One finite verb takes at most one of these suffixes. “Must be able to fly” requires another predication or connective rather than `-du-ki` piled onto the root. `më` or `hol` cannot grant consent; neither can `-ho` be inferred merely from ability `-ki`.

## 5. Time, aspect, boundaries

Past `-te` and future `-yu` locate an action relative to the chosen reference time; aspect `-se` portrays ongoing action, `-mo` completion, `-në` habit. `sen sa goro-te-se.` = They were flying; `sen sa goro-te-mo.` = They had finished flying. “Was flying” does not entail arrival, and “finished flying” does not automatically mean landing somewhere safe. Auxiliary phase particles for imminence, recent completion, continuance and cessation (as established in Chapters 38+) refine the reference-time interpretation; they do not replace the core suffix order.

## 6. If, provided that, and the counterfactual

`wë` sets a condition: `sen wë sa goro-yu, mi sa-m tika-yu.` = If they fly, I will see them. The condition is **not** an assertion that the flight will happen. `bëna` means “as long as/provided that,” conventionally invoking an ongoing condition rather than a one-time hypothetical. An English “if I had flown, I would have seen you” requires a past conditional frame, plus an inferred/projected consequence; the original standard uses tense and context to distinguish this from open conditional futures.

**Important correction of a common over-reading:** the evidential `ye` means inferred/non-witnessed, not “this sentence is definitely counterfactual.” In conditional explanations, `ye` can support a projected or conjectural reading but does not itself encode impossible-world semantics. For high-stakes counterfactuals, explicitly include a statement about what actually occurred in a separate marked sentence.

## 7. Because, so that, although

`ro` introduces an explanatory reason; `ko` introduces an intended purpose; `uro` adds an expectation-defying concession. The distinction is causal vs purposive: “I stayed because you were there” reports a reason; “I stayed so that you would be safe” reports an intended outcome, not guaranteed success. In translation, don't use `ko` as if it asserted fulfillment. Similarly “although” introduces a contrary expectation but does not mean the second clause is false.

## 8. Evidentials and degrees of confidence

The established forms include `si` directly witnessed, `hu` reported, `ye` inferred, plus retrospective `shëku`, instrument/measurement `wërusha`, archival `rëhu`, and customary `lëshu`. At most one evidential belongs to a finite clause. `sëki` and `qëru` express confidence and follow the optional evidential. The mirative `yëqo` precedes evidential marking. Confidence is not certainty, and an evidential is not an endorsement of truth.

Example: `zher sa goro-te ye sëki.` = I tentatively infer they flew. The doubtful register `zher` frames the interpersonal statement but does not replace the more specific low-confidence particle.

## 9. Presupposition and common ground

A noun phrase with `shënao` (same one) presupposes a recoverable comparison target; `dëna` (this) points to a near or salient entity. Corrections (`qëlo`) publicly revise a prior utterance. A translator must preserve whether information is **asserted**, **presupposed**, **inferred**, **quoted**, or **suggested**. If a Qaroshi sentence is a question, it does not assert the positive answer.

## 10. Worked diagnostic contrasts

1. `sen mi kima-m nu këmë.` = I do not drink water. It does not say why.
2. `hë ti kima-m këmë he?` = Do you drink water? It is a question, not an assertion.
3. `qor mi ti-m daru-yu.` = I will protect you. A solemn promise; not evidence of future fulfillment.
4. `sen sa goro-te hu.` = They flew, according to a report; speaker need not have observed it.
5. `sen sa goro-te si.` = They flew, I witnessed it; reports a different evidence source.
6. `sen sa goro-ho.` = They may fly; permission does not imply ability.
7. `sen sa goro-ki.` = They can fly; ability does not imply authorization.
8. `më pëla ti mi-wa nëra ya.` = Please stay with me; polite request, not a command to give up autonomy.

The appropriate final check is a **truth-conditional paraphrase** in English plus a separate note about resonance, evidence, focus and social implication. That audit catches many deceptively fluent mistranslations.
