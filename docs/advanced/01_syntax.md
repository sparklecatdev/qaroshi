# Qaroshi advanced syntax: a construction grammar

Qaroshi's **written resonance marker** is mandatory for each independent sentence. It scopes over every subordinate clause inside that sentence. The marker indicates an interpersonal stance, not grammatical person, truth value, or permission. A sentence lacking it is a quotation fragment, a heading, a label, or nonstandard shorthand; it is not a fully marked independent assertion.

This chapter gives explicit phrase structures, grammaticality tests, and resolution strategies. It supplements Chapters 1–70 without changing their documented morphology. In the examples, brackets `[ ]` show constituency for the reader only. Hyphens show suffix boundaries; neither notation introduces a native character.

## 1. Clause skeleton and precedence

The unmarked order is `RESONANCE — [STANCE] — [TOPIC] — [TIME] — SUBJECT — [INDIRECT ARGUMENTS] — [OBJECT] — [ADVERBIALS] — PREDICATE — [MIRATIVE] — [EVIDENTIAL] — [CONFIDENCE] — [FINAL]`. Ordering before the predicate can shift when the focused relation remains identifiable, especially because a noun's overt case suffix survives movement. The predicate of an ordinary finite clause is **final relative to its arguments**, but clausal evidence and question particles follow it.

Four practical constraints are stronger than preferred word order: (a) a written resonance opens each independent sentence; (b) `nu` immediately precedes the verb or adjective predicate it negates; (c) verbal suffixes follow `root–voice–tense–aspect–modality–manner`; (d) `he` and `ya` select different sentence acts and never close the same clause together.

- `sen mi kima-m këmë.` = I drink water. (Neutral S–O–V.)
- `sen kima-m-së mi këmë.` = As for the water, I drink it. (Topical object keeps its object marker.)
- `më pëla mi-wa nëra ya.` = Please stay with me. (Request with omitted addressee.)
- `hë ti kima-m këmë he?` = Do you drink water? (Question terminal follows predicate.)

**Diagnostic:** replace the pre-verbal object with a pronoun and preserve `-m`; if the result becomes ambiguous, maintain canonical order. Case-marked topicalization is not a license to omit essential arguments when the interpretation would change.

## 2. Constituent boundaries

A noun phrase (NP) has an ordered structure `[(possessor)-na] [(demonstrative)] HEAD [(adjective)] [(degree/numeral)] [derivation] [diminutive] [plural] [case] [topic]`. The noun's suffixes attach to the lexical **head**, not to a free-standing preceding adjective. The possessive NP precedes its head: `mi-na naro` = my home. A noun with no plural marking is unspecified for number unless a numeral or context decides.

`mara-lë-ri-m` is one morphologically complex form, not a prepositional phrase. It means dear friends when functioning as a direct object. `mara-ri-m-së` adds topic after case. By contrast, `mara-ri-lo` indicates location among friends; adding another case would violate the single-case rule.

Relative clauses stand **before** the noun they modify and close with `sha`. A gap in the relative clause corresponds to the noun outside it: `[mi tika-te sha] mara` = the friend I saw. The understood object is inside the relative, so it is **not** pronounced as an additional `mara-m` before `sha`. In a matrix sentence, the whole relative NP receives the matrix case: `më mi [mi tika-te sha] mara-m nashi.` = I cherish the friend I saw. It is never necessary to introduce a second independent resonance in the relative clause.

## 3. Argument structure and semantic roles

A **verb frame** specifies how many participants an event requires. Qaroshi distinguishes `V0` (no object normally expressed), `V1` (subject plus direct object), and `V2` (subject, object, and recipient or goal). These are **lexical tendencies**, not mechanical rules deduced from an English dictionary gloss.

| Pattern | Frame | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| `S V` | motion or state | `sen sa goro.` | They fly. |
| `S O-m V` | perception | `sen mi ti-m tika.` | I see you. |
| `S R-tu O-m V` | transfer | `sen mi mara-tu kima-m pëli.` | I give a friend water. |
| `S SOURCE-de GOAL-gu V` | travel | `sen mi naro-de naro-gu gura.` | I travel from one home toward a home. |
| `S ADJ` | property predicate | `sen ti haro.` | You are well. |

The repeated `naro` in the directional example refers to **two contextually distinct homes**; without context a clarifying demonstrative is preferable. `-wa` means accompaniment, not automatically instrument; `-shi` marks instrument and can mark the agent in some passives. The basic semantic roles are agent (initiator), patient (affected entity), recipient (`-tu`), source (`-de`), goal (`-gu`), location (`-lo`) and accompaniment (`-wa`). The direct-object marker is `-m`, not English word order alone.

## 4. Voice, case, and recoverability

Voice suffixes apply before tense: `daru-li-te` = was protected; `goro-ru` = cause to fly; `nashi-wo` = love each other; `tika-ne` = see oneself. An ordinary finite verb selects at most one voice suffix, including in passive constructions. A passive can omit the agent. If agent identification is essential, prefer a separate explicit clause when `-shi` might also be interpreted as a tool: `sen mara naro-m daru-te. sen naro daru-li-te.` = A friend protected the home. The home was protected.

A causative with multiple named participants requires a clear causee and effected participant. It should not be assumed that an English causative maps one-to-one onto `-ru`. A translator can use two finite clauses when the causee could be mistaken for the patient.

## 5. Ellipsis and zero subjects

Subjects may be omitted in immediate conversational replies if the speaker and referent are recoverable. Example: `hë ti haro he?` / `më haro.` = Are you well? / I'm well. This ellipsis is **licensed by discourse**, not a new first-person verb ending. For legal, scientific or technical prose, repeat the subject after a topic change. An omitted addressee in a command, `më pëla nëra ya.`, usually refers to the intended listener. A standalone dialogue reply still begins with a resonance marker, even when it is one adjective or verb.

## 6. Clause joining and nominalization

`ara` links coordinated events; `nao` marks contrast; `ro` gives reason; `ko` gives purpose; `wë` introduces a condition; `uro` concessive expectation; `do` ends a propositional complement. The conjunction or complementizer does not replace a predicate. Each conjunct is independently meaningful, but only the first clause in a written **sentence** receives the resonance marker.

- `sen mi kima-m këmë ara sa naro-lo nëra.` = I drink water and they stay home.
- `sen sa haro do mi sëmi.` = I know that they are well. (`do` marks the boundary of the claim known.)
- `më ti mi-wa nëra, ko mi haro.` = Stay with me so that I feel safe.

When subordinating several clauses, nesting must have a unique parse. A three-clause chain should use repeated content boundaries or be separated into two sentences if two attachment sites remain possible. English commas are only a Romanized teaching aid; disambiguation comes from the connectives and predicate boundaries.

## 7. Relative clauses and the gap test

A relative clause can describe a subject, object or location. A relative gap is inferred through the frame and case roles. With the base `tika` (see), `[mi tika-te sha] mara` interprets `mara` as what I saw because `mi` already occupies the subject slot. If multiple plausible gaps remain, rewrite with an ordinary standalone sentence and an overt demonstrative rather than assuming a universal English relative pronoun.

Good editorial translation: `më mi [mi tika-te sha] mara-m nashi.` = I cherish the friend I saw. Potentially unclear: a relative clause with both subject and object omitted. Preferred repair: state the participant with an overt pronoun, then refer to the same one with a demonstrative in the following sentence. This is especially important for instructions, policy, and emergency communication.

## 8. Wh-questions and embedded questions

A direct yes/no or content question has final `he`. A question word stays in the position that its answer occupies: `hë ti khe-m shëma he?` = What do you eat? `hë ti lona nëra he?` = Where do you stay? `hë ti shoro wori he?` = Why do you explore? The question particle is not identical to the curiosity resonance `hë`.

An **embedded question** is a nominal proposition, not a second independent question sentence. In writing, it inherits the outer resonance. A question embedded under `sharoa` (ask) or `sëmi` (know) should remain distinguishable from the surrounding main question. When two final `he` particles might compete for interpretation, use a direct quoted question in a separate sentence. The quotation convention must be used consistently; do not place an unmarked resonance inside a quote and present it as independent standard writing.

## 9. Binding, deixis, and pronoun choice

`mi` = I, `ti` = you, `sa` = singular they; `naru` includes the addressee and `muru` excludes them; `tiri` and `sari` are plural. Do not translate both `naru` and `muru` with an undifferentiated English “we” in contexts where the inclusion distinction affects consent, invitations or membership. The demonstratives `dëna`, `tënaro`, `rënao` are anchored to the speaker/listener spatial relationship and need not express emotional distance.

`-ne` on the predicate is the ordinary reflexive; do not add an object `mi-m` merely to force an English “myself” translation when a reflexive form already encodes that role. Pronoun reference over long stretches should be renewed with an overt noun or demonstrative, particularly after more than one singular `sa` referent appears.

## 10. Grammaticality and repair checklist

For each new sentence: identify independent sentence boundaries; check one mandatory resonance per boundary; locate its finite predicate; count argument slots from the verb frame; verify case marking; verify suffix order and mutual exclusions; check negation adjacency; attach question/request/evidence particles; draw clause boundaries; finally paraphrase in English to see whether scope and referents survive. A sentence may be morphologically possible but pragmatically misleading; those are different diagnoses.

**Avoid claims of exhaustive formal coverage.** This reference specifies the productive standard constructions, not an automatic parser with complete knowledge of every conceivable human meaning. The corpus contains controlled editorial utterances rather than recordings of independent speakers.
