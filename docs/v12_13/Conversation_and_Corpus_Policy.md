# Qaroshi 12.0: Conversation, naturalness, and corpus evidence

## New material, what was actually written

The Qaroshi 12.0 editorial volume contains **54 separately scripted conversations**, **434 turns**, and approximately **1,866 Qaroshi tokens**, each conversation organized around its own conflict or communicative task. Added to the previously preserved **49** Qaroshi 11.0 scenes, the library holds **103** distinct editorial scenarios. The new dialogues include borrowing and repairing a compass, negotiating consent over art, correcting a rumor, making limited promises, asking for clinical advice instead of guessing, listening after an argument, and distinguishing a source report from direct observation.

The edition also includes **18 original narrative/essay pieces**, **255 complete Qaroshi sentences**, and **1,069 Qaroshi tokens**. Genres include fictional narrative, reflective essays, an epistolary passage, a weather report, a diary, ethical argument and a language-learning essay. They are small connected texts, not padded copies of the earlier 26-turn practice template.

Across the **689 new sentences** from both datasets, **437 distinct sentence strings** occur. Some core sentences repeat across works, reflecting deliberately conservative grammar. This is a real limitation of lexical and stylistic diversity, and should be addressed through broader writing styles, not hidden behind the total count.

## What 12.0 does *not* establish

The earlier 123,000 Qaroshi tokens in the 8–10 release remain mostly controlled schoolroom practice. Adding a few thousand more editorial tokens does **not** fulfill the 100,000-word diverse naturalistic corpus goal. The new scenes have coherent distinct premises, but they are **not spontaneous conversations recorded from independent human speakers**. Neither text volume nor correctness tests establish community-wide idiomatic habits.

## Naturalness and register

A useful dialogue has a social reason for each utterance. For example, a visitor asks permission to enter rather than interpreting an unlocked door as consent. A witness corrects a rumor by saying how information was learned. A friend can refuse a flight tenderly without weakening the refusal. The 16 resonance markers frame the speaker's stance in every independent written sentence, including informal text messages; a tone shift can be expressed by starting a new sentence with a new marker.

Silence, ellipsis and one-word reactions may occur in conversational fragments, but they cannot be silently used to remove resonance from a complete independent written clause. Proposed slang, reductions and abbreviations should receive a separate dated editorial status until spontaneous community use supports them.

## Parallel translation risk

Contextual English may add a subject from the previous turn, infer motivation or expand a literal sentence. That can help a student understand the narrative, but it is not the same as literal grammar. The release checks the 689 new sentences for visible morphology and common translation hazards. **103 sentences** currently carry at least one review flag; many reflect incomplete literal parser coverage or a contextually implied rather than explicit feature. See `data/v12_13/dialogue_literature_translation_qa.csv` for all flags. Passing this heuristic check is not a human translation validation.

## To build a future genuinely naturalistic corpus

Solicit writers with different personal styles and kinds of speech situations. Tag each contribution with genre, original author, date, license, edition, intended resonance, source-vs-translated text, review status, and approval evidence. Maintain two counts: actual Qaroshi tokens (excluding English glosses and duplicated examples) and unique documents. Quality audits should examine conversation continuity, argument roles, coreference, stance consistency, semantic selection and register. For human recordings, follow informed consent and privacy rules; do not publish personal chats without permission.
